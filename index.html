Tips for a great CV

A quick, practical checklist — not rules, just what tends to work.

Keep it to one page

  • Recruiters often spend well under a minute on a first pass — a tight one-pager reads faster than a sprawling one.
  • One page is the norm for most candidates; two pages is reasonable once you're well into a senior or executive career.
  • To cut length: trim roles older than ~10–15 years to a line each, drop bullets that don't support the job you want, and tighten wording instead of shrinking the font.
  • Try the ATS Compact or Minimal template if you're fighting for space — they fit more content per inch than the two-column layouts.

Get past the ATS (the software that screens most CVs first)

  • Use conventional section titles — Experience, Education, Skills — parsers look for these; creative alternatives can go unread.
  • Mirror the language of the job post: match its skill names, tools, and job title wording so keyword matching finds them.
  • Spell out acronyms at least once (e.g. "Search Engine Optimization (SEO)") so both the long and short form match.
  • If you suspect strict/older parsing software, prefer the ATS Compact template — no columns, icons, or graphics that can confuse a parser.
  • Always export as a real PDF (or DOCX) with selectable text, not a scanned image or screenshot.

Then write for the human who reads it next

  • Start bullets with a strong action verb — Led, Built, Reduced, Launched — instead of "Responsible for."
  • Quantify impact wherever you can: percentages, dollars, time saved, team size, users reached.
  • Keep each bullet to one or two lines; if it doesn't support the role you're applying for, cut it.
  • Tailor your title and summary to the job — a generic summary reads as not-quite-relevant.

Formatting hygiene

  • List experience in reverse-chronological order (most recent first).
  • Keep tense consistent — past roles in past tense, your current role in present tense.
  • Stick to one or two accent colors and a single font family; consistency reads as more polished than variety.
  • Proofread — typos are one of the most common, and most avoidable, reasons a CV gets passed over.

Contact details

  • Include email, phone, and a city/region — a full street address is rarely expected anymore and mostly just a privacy risk.
  • A portfolio, GitHub, or LinkedIn link is worth including whenever it's actually relevant to the role.
